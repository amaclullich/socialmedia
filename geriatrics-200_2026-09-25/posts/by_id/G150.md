# G150: A low fall can be major trauma

- Category: C15 Hospital care, emergency care and discharge
- Audience: practitioners
- Series: Teaching round
- Post type: evidence_interpretation
- Visual format: carousel (comparison then bars)
- Status: text text_final, visual None
- Working id: C15-P03 (candidate C15-11)

## Insight

By 2013 the commonest cause of major trauma in England and Wales was a fall from less than 2 metres, with about 1 in 4 cases over 75. After a low fall in an older person, look for head and chest injury whatever the height.

## Angle and position

Trauma systems were built around road collisions in younger people. Registry data show the mechanism has changed, and rib fractures and bleeding inside the skull are common enough in older people after a fall that the height of the fall is a poor guide. Position: look at the injury and the person, not the height.

Basis: mixed: the registry shift, the rib fracture figures, the under-triage reviews and the 1 in 20 bleeding estimate are empirical findings with stated limits; the instruction to look for head and chest injury after any low fall is clinical judgement. No claim is made about anticoagulants (the evidence does not support one).

## LinkedIn (1789 characters)

```text
By 2013, the commonest cause of major trauma recorded in the national trauma registry for England and Wales was a fall from less than 2 metres: 39 in 100 cases. In 1990 it was road collisions: 59 in 100 cases. In the national trauma registry, the average age of people with major trauma rose from 36 to 54 over those 23 years, and by 2013 about 1 in 4 were over 75.

Two cautions. The figures stop in 2013 and should not be read as current. Some of the shift may reflect changes in which hospitals submitted data and a rise in CT scanning (from 34 to 87 in 100 cases), which finds more injuries.

Three further findings are about injury in older people. Each has limits, and only the bleeding estimate concerns people who fell.

Rib fractures first. This was one US trauma centre, and the injuries came from any blunt chest injury. People aged 65 or over admitted with rib fractures died in hospital more often than younger adults with similar injuries (22 in 100 versus 10 in 100) and got pneumonia more often (31 in 100 versus 17 in 100). Each extra broken rib was linked with higher odds of death. The data were published in 2000.

Head injury: across studies, about 1 in 20 people aged 65 or over who came to an emergency department after a fall had bleeding inside the skull. Studies varied in who was scanned, and most were at high risk of bias.

Triage: reviews of ambulance triage rules, mostly from North America, find they miss serious injury in older people more often than across all ages combined. Whether that changes outcomes is still uncertain.

A low fall can still cause serious injury in an older person. These findings do not say that every low fall needs a trauma call. We should look for head and chest injury after a low fall, and judge by the injury and the person.
```

## Attention and follow potential

Pause: The most common cause of major trauma is now a fall from standing height, which a mechanism-based picture of trauma does not suggest.

Gain: A reason to examine for head and chest injury after a low fall, with the size of the evidence and its age stated.

Follow: Registry and trial figures with the year, the setting and the limits kept next to the numbers.
