# G198: Exclusion should need a reason

- Category: C20 Ageism, research exclusion, uncertainty and measurement
- Audience: practitioners
- Series: Who gets left out
- Post type: criticism
- Visual format: carousel
- Status: text text_final, visual None
- Working id: C20-P07 (candidate C20-03)

## Insight

Adults who lack capacity to consent are often left out of research although the law in England and Wales already allows their inclusion with safeguards. Few protocols give a reason for excluding them.

## Angle and position

Reverse the default: studies of conditions common in later life should include adults who lack capacity unless the protocol gives a scientific reason. Stays on default exclusion of people who cannot consent; no trial-population points from other posts.

Basis: mixed: empirical findings (Taylor 2012 review; Shepherd 2019 registry review; Shepherd 2024 framework; England and Wales legal route) and ethical judgement (include unless a scientific reason is given), marked as judgement.

## LinkedIn (1708 characters)

```text
Many studies leave out people with cognitive impairment or adults who lack capacity to consent for themselves. Figures from two different settings show how often, and the law is the third point.

1. In a review of 434 original studies in one leading geriatrics journal over two years (2008 and 2009), at least 127 (29%) explicitly excluded people with cognitive impairment. Very few gave any justification.
2. Among about 80 UK trials completed between 2015 and 2018 in conditions where impaired capacity is common, 15 allowed adults who lacked capacity to take part. That search of a trial registry was not comprehensive.
3. In England and Wales, the law already allows inclusion. Drug trials follow the Clinical Trials Regulations 2004, with a legal representative's consent. Other research follows the Mental Capacity Act 2005, with the advice of a consultee (someone who knows the person and says what they would want).

The authors of a UK framework paper say adults with impaired capacity are frequently excluded, partly because researchers are unfamiliar with the legal and ethical frameworks. The UK INCLUDE framework for trials with people who lack capacity to consent gives trial teams four key questions and worksheets for designing trials that include them. Its effect on inclusion has not yet been evaluated.

This is a judgement, not a finding: we should include adults who lack capacity in studies of conditions common in later life, with the legal safeguards, unless the protocol gives a scientific reason not to. Exclusion without a stated reason is unfair to them, and it leaves their care with less evidence.

When you review a protocol, ask whether each exclusion is justified in writing.
```

## Sources and verification

- C20-03-a (supported_with_qualification): Of 434 JAGS research articles 2008-09, at least 29% explicitly excluded people with cognitive impairment; 6% gave justification; 14% discussed as limitation.
  - Source: Taylor JS, DeMers SM, Vig EK, Borson S. The disappearing subject: exclusion of people with cognitive impairment and dementia from geriatrics research. J Am Geriatr Soc. 2012;60(3):413-9.
  - Passage: "Of 434 articles examined, 16% used recruitment methods likely to reduce participation by persons with cognitive impairment. At least 29% of studies (n = 127) employed explicit exclusion criteria. ... Few (6%) provided any justification for exclusion criteria used, only 43% gave any reason for exclusion, and only 14% discussed exclusion as a possible limitation." (Abstract, Results)
- C20-03-b (supported_with_qualification): Few recently completed UK trials in conditions linked with cognitive impairment included adults who lacked capacity.
  - Source: Shepherd V, Wood F, Griffith R, Sheehan M, Hood K. Protection by exclusion? The (lack of) inclusion of adults who lack capacity to consent to research in clinical trials in the UK. Trials. 2019;20(1):474.
  - Passage: "We identified 81 trials in the conditions or populations searched for which were completed between 2015 and 2018, of which 15 trials (18%) allowed for the inclusion of adults who lacked capacity" (Full text, Main text (abstract says 80 retrieved trials))
- C20-03-c (supported_with_qualification): UK law (England and Wales) allows adults lacking capacity to take part in research with safeguards via consultees/legal representatives.
  - Source: Shepherd V, Wood F, Griffith R, Sheehan M, Hood K. Protection by exclusion? The (lack of) inclusion of adults who lack capacity to consent to research in clinical trials in the UK. Trials. 2019;20(1):474.
  - Passage: "In England and Wales, the legal frameworks govern the inclusion of adults who lack capacity in clinical trials of medicinal products under the Medicines for Human Use (Clinical Trials) Regulations 2004 [], with other types of research governed by the Mental Capacity Act 2005 []. Under this legal framework, someone who knows the person who lacks capacity is approached by the researcher to act as their legal representative or consultee." (Full text, Background)

  - Source: Shepherd V, Joyce K, Lewis A, Flynn S, Clout M, Nocivelli B, et al. Improving the inclusion of an under-served group in trials: development and implementation of the INCLUDE Impaired Capacity to Consent Framework. Trials. 2024;25(1):83.

## Attention and follow potential

Pause: Researchers and ethics reviewers recognise the default; the lack of stated reasons and the 15 of about 80 figure are hard to defend.

Gain: The legal routes in one place and a test to apply to any protocol: is each exclusion justified?

Follow: The account takes research exclusion seriously with specific evidence rather than general complaint.
