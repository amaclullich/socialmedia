# G153: A chest infection in a care home: hospital, or treatment where they live?

- Category: C15 Hospital care, emergency care and discharge
- Audience: public
- Series: Weighing it up
- Post type: decision_support
- Visual format: annotated scene plus comparison (2 slides)
- Status: text text_final, visual None
- Working id: C15-P06 (candidate C15-23)

## Insight

In a Canadian trial in 22 nursing homes, about 10 in 100 residents with a chest infection such as pneumonia who were treated in the home on a pathway with close monitoring went to hospital, against 22 in 100 with usual care. The trial did not show a clear difference in deaths.

## Angle and position

The choice is often presented as hospital or nothing. One trial shows a third option that worked when the home had extra monitoring. The decision still depends on how unwell the person is, what they want and what the home can provide. Position: treatment in the home with proper monitoring is a reasonable option to discuss, and the person's wishes belong in the decision.

Basis: mixed: the percentages and the content of the pathway are empirical findings from one trial; that the option is worth discussing, and that the person's wishes and the home's ability to monitor should shape the decision, is judgement.

Treated in the care home, about 10 in 100 residents with a chest infection such as pneumonia (an infection of the lungs) went to hospital in a Canadian trial. With usual care it was 22 in 100. That is 12 fewer in every 100.

The trial ran in 22 nursing homes in Ontario between 2001 and 2005. It included 680 residents aged 65 or over with a chest infection, including pneumonia. It compared treating residents in the home with a set plan of care against usual care.

Home treatment meant antibiotic tablets, chest X-rays taken in the home, oxygen level checks, fluids, and close checks by a research nurse.

About 8 in 100 treated in the home and 9 in 100 with usual care died. The trial did not show a clear difference in deaths, quality of life or everyday functioning. The deaths figure is uncertain: the trial could not rule out more deaths, or fewer, in either group.

The limits are real. It is a single trial, now old, run in one Canadian region. The close monitoring came from a research nurse. Whether the same approach works in homes without that monitoring is a judgement, not a finding. The decision also depends on how unwell the person is, what they want, and what the home can provide.

If you or a relative live in a care home, ask what care is possible there if you become unwell, and say what you would want. We should have that conversation before an illness starts.

Verification notes: C15-23-a, -b, -c: Loeb 2006 abstract. Population is lower respiratory tract infection including pneumonia, so the captions say 'chest infection such as pneumonia'; 12 percentage points is the cluster-adjusted difference; deaths 'about 8 and 9 in 100', not 'no effect'. 'Nursing home' kept for the trial setting and 'care home' used for UK readers; the pathway applicability to UK homes is framed as judgement. The sentence 'not designed to show a difference in deaths' comes from the qualification (not powered). Drawing details (people, objects) are illustration, not claims.

## Attention and follow potential

Pause: Many people assume a chest infection in a care home always means an ambulance and an admission.

Gain: The numbers, what home treatment involved, and a question to ask the care home.

Follow: Treatment choices set out with the figures per 100 and the limits of the evidence.
