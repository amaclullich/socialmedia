# G199: Long-term conditions arrive earlier in deprived areas

- Category: C20 Ageism, research exclusion, uncertainty and measurement
- Audience: both
- Series: Who gets left out
- Post type: criticism
- Visual format: diagram
- Status: text text_final, visual None
- Working id: C20-P08 (candidate C20-14)

## Insight

In a study of 1.75 million people in Scotland, having two or more long-term conditions became common 10 to 15 years earlier in the most deprived areas than in the most affluent. As an implication, a fixed starting age for a service or study may reach people in deprived areas later in the course of their conditions.

## Angle and position

An age threshold looks neutral. Revised angle: built on Barnett 2012 alone (population level, 2007 data), no healthy life expectancy figures, no named age-gated policy; need-based entry offered as judgement.

Basis: mixed: empirical finding (Barnett 2012, cross-sectional, population level), interpretation (what a fixed starting age implies, marked as an implication) and ethical judgement (do not rely on age alone as the way in), marked as judgement.

Two or more long-term conditions became common at ages 10 to 15 years younger in the most deprived areas of Scotland than in the most affluent. That comes from a cross-sectional study of 1.75 million people registered with 314 Scottish general practices in March 2007. It compares age groups at one point in time rather than following people.

It describes whole populations, so each person's course will differ. In the same study, slightly more people with two or more long-term conditions were under 65 (210,500) than 65 or over (194,996). The proportion was highest in older people.

## LinkedIn (1285 characters)

```text
Two or more long-term conditions became common at ages 10 to 15 years younger in the most deprived areas of Scotland than in the most affluent.

That comes from a study of 1,751,841 people registered with 314 Scottish general practices, using data from March 2007. It describes whole populations, so each person's course will differ. It is cross-sectional, so it compares age groups at one point in time.

The same study found that slightly more people with two or more long-term conditions were under 65 (210,500) than 65 or over (194,996), although the proportion was highest in older people.

One implication is offered here as interpretation. A service or study that opens at a fixed age may reach people in the most deprived areas later in the course of their conditions. At the same age, people in deprived areas may be further into their conditions.

This is a judgement: services and studies for older people should not rely on age alone to decide who is eligible. If the aim is to reach people who need them, eligibility based on need fits better. It would also give more attention to people who are poorly served. Need could mean, for example, how much help a person needs with everyday tasks.

When you see an age threshold on a service or a study, ask whom it reaches late.
```

## Attention and follow potential

Pause: The size of the gap, in years, surprises most readers, and the diagram turns it into a consequence for any age-based entry point.

Gain: A concrete reason to judge services and studies by need rather than birth date.

Follow: The account pays attention to people who are poorly served, with Scottish data.
