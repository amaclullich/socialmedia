# House style for the geriatrics 200 collection

Version 1, 25 September 2026. Compiled from Alasdair MacLullich's house rules (the plain-english, paper-to-post, research-infographics, socialbee-plain-english-audit and teaching-comics skills; the FABLE hub 00_START_HERE rules; the 6 September 2026 X profile analysis) and the brief for this collection. Where this file and a dated ruling from Alasdair disagree, his ruling wins.

The one access limitation to know: the master banned-words file (`AI writing platform/01-Core Anti-AI Rules/BANNED_WORDS_MASTER_July2026.md`) was not reachable from this environment. The banned lists below are the union of every list in the skills and briefs that were reachable.

## 1. What the collection is for

200 original posts on geriatrics, excluding delirium as a topic, for X, LinkedIn, Bluesky and Threads. Each post must:

1. Be factually accurate, with every substantive claim checked against a source in the evidence library.
2. Give a practitioner, an older person or a carer something useful: an action, a distinction, an explanation, a question or an insight.
3. Have an interesting, specific angle.
4. Take a defensible position where the subject supports one.
5. Open with a first line, and carry a visual, strong enough to make someone stop scrolling.
6. Show why this account is worth following for more careful, useful material.

Aim for posts people would save, send to a colleague or relative, or remember in a clinical encounter.

## 2. Audiences

Every post has a primary audience: `practitioners`, `public` (older people, families and carers) or `both`. The collection needs substantial coverage for practitioners and for older people and carers. Address older people directly ("you") where it fits. Do not write about older people as if none of them will read the post.

## 3. Values, expressed through specifics

These principles guide the editorial position. Express them through observations, arguments and practical implications. Never as slogans.

- Every person has equal dignity, including people with advanced illness, dementia, disability or profound dependence.
- A person's worth does not depend on productivity, wealth, independence, cognitive ability or how easy they are to care for.
- Give particular attention to people who are poor, isolated, overlooked, excluded or poorly served.
- Support solidarity between generations and shared responsibility for care.
- Respect older people's agency, relationships, preferences and right to take part in decisions.
- Value families, unpaid carers and local communities. Support them without assuming unlimited unpaid labour.
- Respect care workers and practitioners. Examine the conditions that help or obstruct good care.
- Support fair access, responsible use of resources and services organised around human needs.
- Good care can include rehabilitation, symptom relief, companionship, personal care and support through dying.
- Distinguish a person's continuing worth from the likely benefit or burden of a particular treatment. Avoid both therapeutic abandonment and burdensome treatment without a reasonable prospect of benefit.

Never mention religion, religious institutions or theological terms. Never state or imply Alasdair's position on a disputed policy question beyond these principles. Never blame whole professions. Never make a patient or carer the joke; dry humour at the expense of systems, myths or routines is fine.

## 4. Voice

Clinically literate, direct, humane, sceptical of overclaiming, willing to challenge poor practice. The account's best-performing posts combine a concrete, recognisable situation, a clear correction of meaning, and a practical instruction. Keep that structure available; do not use it every time.

- Plain British English. UK spelling (organise, haemoglobin, oesophagus, programme, anaemia, orthopaedic, paediatric, ageing, behaviour, centre, disorientated).
- Concrete words, people doing things, literal verbs (is, has, does, gets, causes, helps, shows, finds, needs, stops, starts).
- Vary sentence length. One idea per sentence. For the public, aim under 20 words, never past 25. For practitioners, never past 35.
- Short paragraphs of one or two sentences, separated by blank lines.
- Preserve justified uncertainty without filling every post with caveats.
- Take a position when the evidence supports one, and say it directly.
- No invented anecdotes. No first-person clinical experience ("I have seen", "on my ward", "a patient of mine"). No invented patients, relatives, quotations, testimonials or scenes presented as real. A generic second-person situation ("If your father has been in bed for three days") is allowed when it is true of many people and adds no invented detail. A clearly labelled illustrative example ("An illustrative example, not a real patient:") is allowed sparingly.
- Never claim anything about Alasdair's own practice, audit work, teaching or opinions beyond what is in a published source.

## 5. Plain English (the house standard)

The text must be understood at first reading by a tired, worried relative reading on a phone with no medical training. For practitioner posts a technical term may stand without a plain equivalent when every likely reader knows it; everything else still applies.

- Every term that carries meaning is replaced by the plain word, or kept with the plain meaning beside it at first use. Examples: polypharmacy (taking many medicines); sarcopenia (loss of muscle strength and mass); orthostatic hypotension (a drop in blood pressure on standing); deprescribing (stopping or reducing medicines that no longer help); comprehensive geriatric assessment (a full assessment of health, function, medicines, mood and support by a specialist team).
- Statistical shorthand is cut for the public: hazard ratio, odds ratio, confidence interval, noninferior, AUC. Say what it means.
- Every number gets a meaning in the same or the next sentence: a comparison, a denominator, a time period, or a plain reading of the scale. Natural frequencies ("about 1 in 3 people over 65") beat bare percentages.
- The result that went the other way is stated as plainly as the headline.
- Two figures for one effect are reconciled in plain words, or only one is given.
- The last sentence says something the reader can do or could disagree with. Never an empty close.
- State risk as a number and a comparison, not as drama. No false reassurance, no cheerful endings, no "you are not alone".

## 6. Banned words and phrases

Never use (quotations of other people's verbatim words excepted):

cannot (recast, or "can't" in speech), plainly, crucial, vital, delve, journey, landscape, robust, leverage, matter / matters / mattered / what matters (except the fixed term "What Matters" in the 4Ms framework), the shape of, lives in (abstract subject), sits in / sits alongside (abstract subject), lands (figurative), quiet / quietly (figurative), load-bearing, load bearing, heavy lifting, carries weight, earns its place, the honest answer, worth saying, worth noting, it is important to note, the whole point, honestly (as self-authentication), exactly / absolutely (as agreement), step by step, short answer, here's, here is (as an announcement), here's the thing, the key is, key takeaway, let that sink in, game-changer, game changing, breakthrough, proves, unpopular opinion, ultimately, in essence, the bottom line, furthermore, moreover, underscore(s), highlights the importance of, plays a role in, sheds light on, paves the way, simply put, wake-up call, red flag, silver lining, tip of the iceberg, at the heart of, front line, roadmap, toolkit, spotlight, perfect storm, ticking time bomb, silver tsunami, burden (when describing older people as a burden to society; "treatment burden" and "pill burden" as technical terms are allowed), elderly (as a noun: "the elderly"), senior citizens, pensioners (as a group label, except in official statistics names such as "pensioner poverty").

Prefer "older people" or "older adults". Use a person's condition as a description, not an identity ("a man with dementia", not "a dementia patient").

## 7. Banned shapes

- Negation then correction: "This isn't X, it's Y"; "X is not A. It is B." State B directly. (One exception: correcting a specific, named false belief is allowed when the belief is quoted as a belief, e.g. "Many people think falls are a normal part of ageing. Most falls have causes that can be found." Use sparingly.)
- Self-answered setup questions ("The result? ...", "Why? Because ...").
- A closing moral sentence beginning "Because".
- Paired or mirrored aphorisms and epigram closers.
- Engagement bait ("Agree?", "Thoughts?", "Comment below", "Tag someone", "You won't believe", "Read that again").
- Announcements of what follows ("Here is what to do", "The key point is", "Simply put", "Let me explain").
- Predicate personification: an abstract noun given a verb only a person or moving body could perform ("the data speak", "the evidence points", "frailty hides", "the guideline carries").
- Metaphors, similes and images, including dead ones. Literal phrases first. Use a comparison only when it is crucial to understanding, and then make it literal and accurate.
- Stacked hedges for tone ("may potentially be associated with").
- Exaggerated teasers and concealed answers. Deliver the promised insight within the post.
- "A new study published in", "Imagine", "Just published", "Did you know".

## 8. Formatting

- No em dashes or en dashes used as dashes anywhere, including visuals. No spaced hyphen used as a dash (" - "). Use a comma, colon, semicolon, full stop or new sentence. Number ranges use "to" ("3 to 4").
- No exclamation marks. No emoji. No markdown in captions (no bold markers, headings, arrow bullets). LinkedIn may use a plain numbered list ("1." lines) in about a third of posts; X, Bluesky and Threads do not use lists.
- Open compounds: "care home", "hip fracture", "home care", "day centre", "follow up" as a verb; "follow-up" as a noun keeps its hyphen.
- Hashtags, if any, on a separate final line after a blank line. X 0 to 1, LinkedIn 0 to 3, Bluesky 0 to 1, Threads 0 or 1 topic tag. Most posts need none.
- No conflict of interest declarations. No AI disclosure lines (the visuals in this collection are drawn graphics, not realistic synthetic imagery of people, so no label applies).
- No claims about platform algorithms or engagement tactics anywhere.

## 9. Evidence rules (apply to captions, headlines and visuals equally)

- Every substantive medical claim, recommendation, statistic, and claim about services or policy must be in the evidence library (`evidence/`) with a source that was opened in this project and supports the exact wording.
- Keep distinct: association and causation; relative and absolute effects; screening and diagnosis; an evidence gap and evidence of no benefit; a trial population and the people the post is about; a subgroup and the whole study; adjusted and crude figures.
- Absolute values first ("N in 100"), relative effect second. Differences between percentages are percentage points.
- Never turn an odds ratio or hazard ratio into "X times more likely" or into an absolute difference without valid inputs.
- No causal verbs ("causes", "leads to", "makes", "drives") for observational findings. Say "is linked with" and, where it helps, say the link does not prove cause.
- A non-significant result is not "no effect": say "did not show a clear difference" and give the uncertainty in plain words where it could change the reading.
- Background figures from a paper's introduction are not that paper's findings.
- Admissions are not people. Prescriptions are not patients.
- A moral position does not need a fabricated scientific justification. Mark ethical judgement as judgement ("We should", "It is wrong to"), and do not dress it as a finding.
- Prefer recent evidence for current practice and new findings; use older high-quality evidence where it remains the right source. Journal prestige alone does not establish quality.
- If an essential claim cannot be supported, revise or replace the post. Unresolved items stay out of the finished collection.
- Record how each claim was verified: `full_text` (PMC full text or an opened PDF), `abstract` (PubMed abstract read in this project), `official_document` (an official report or guideline opened through Drive, PubMed or a GitHub copy), or `search_extract` (only a search-engine extract from an official domain). A `search_extract` is not enough on its own for an essential claim. Official web pages (NICE, ONS, NHS, gov.uk) could not be opened from this environment, so prefer claims that published papers or opened documents support.

## 10. Openings and closings

- The first line is concrete and intelligible without context: a precise observation, a defensible claim, a revealing finding, a recognisable situation or a useful question (a real question the reader might ask, not a setup the post answers in the next word).
- Vary openings across the collection. No more than a handful of posts may open with the same construction ("If your...", "Most people...", "A study of...", "When..."). Never open with a statistic followed by "That's..." more than a few times.
- Vary closings: an instruction, a question to ask, a claim to disagree with, a practical test, a named limitation. Avoid ending every post on the limitation.
- Invitations to follow: occasional (roughly 1 post in 10), specific and natural ("More posts in the Weighing it up series take one treatment decision at a time."). Never promise a posting schedule. Never on every caption.

## 11. Recurring series

Each post belongs to one series. Vary the language and visual treatment within a series.

| Series | Purpose | Main audience |
|---|---|---|
| Reading the evidence | What a study or statistic shows, what it does not, and how to read it | practitioners and both |
| Ask this | A specific question an older person or family can ask, and why the answer helps | public |
| Small detail, real effect | An everyday practical detail with a substantial effect on care | both |
| Weighing it up | A treatment decision: benefits, burdens, time to benefit, the person's goals | both |
| What gets counted | The gap between what services measure and what people experience or value | both |
| Teaching round | A clinical teaching point: a distinction, a shortcut that needs qualifying, an examination or history point | practitioners |
| Who gets left out | Exclusion, ageism, poverty, access, people poorly served | both |
| Care that works | A specific example or model of good care and what makes it work | both |

The collection must not be dominated by complaints. Aim for at least a third of posts that explain, teach or show good care rather than criticise.

## 12. Platforms (see `PLATFORMS.md` for verified limits)

Write each platform version at the length the idea needs. The central claim and the necessary qualifications stay the same in all four versions. Never produce a short version by deleting words from a long one: write it from the finding.

- X: Alasdair has X Premium, so there is no 280-character limit. Normal working length is about 400 to 700 characters; longer (up to about 1,400) when the content needs it; shorter than the LinkedIn version.
- LinkedIn: about 900 to 2,200 characters. May add context, a plain numbered list (in about a third of posts), and the source line at the end.
- Bluesky: 300 characters maximum including spaces and any hashtag. Must still make sense on its own: who, what, and what to do or think.
- Threads: 500 characters maximum. Conversational but the same claim.
- Source: every post has a `source_reply`, a short line with the public link or links (DOI link, PubMed link or official page) that can be posted as a reply or first comment. Use public-facing links (doi.org, pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, official sites).

## 13. Visual identity (full spec in `visuals/design_system/`)

- Every post has a finished visual. Formats: statement card, comparison, checklist or question card, diagram (flow, timeline, cycle, cascade), accurate chart (bars, icon arrays), carousel (2 to 4 slides, so the same set posts on X and Bluesky), drawn illustration, short comic strip (3 or 4 panels), annotated scene. Choose the format that best explains the idea.
- Palette: off-white ground #FBFAF6; ink #1F2A30; deep teal #0F3C4F; teal #15535B; mint #DCEBEA; pale teal #B9CDCC; burnt orange #C24E12 (emphasis marks and key numbers on light grounds; never on teal); gold #D9A441 (text only on deep teal); secondary grey #5F6B76; warm sand #EFE6D6 for illustration backgrounds.
- Type: Montserrat 800 for headings, Inter for body. Restrained text, strong contrast, legible at phone size (body text at least 30 px on a 1080 px wide canvas).
- Meaning is never carried by colour alone: use labels, position, shape or pattern as well.
- Charts only from verified data, with labels, denominators and timeframes. Illustrative examples are labelled "Illustrative example" and are visibly distinct from measured results.
- People: respectful, varied depictions of older people, families and practitioners (age, sex, skin tone, body shape, disability, setting). Credible bodies, equipment and care environments. No stock portrayal of every older person as helpless, confused or alone.
- No fabricated quotations or testimonials on visuals. Speech in comics is illustrative dialogue in a clearly illustrative strip, never attributed to a real person.
- No decorative clutter. Every visual contributes an observation, explanation or emotional understanding.
- Credit line on evidence visuals: short source reference (author, journal, year). Footer on every visual: "Alasdair MacLullich" and the series name.
